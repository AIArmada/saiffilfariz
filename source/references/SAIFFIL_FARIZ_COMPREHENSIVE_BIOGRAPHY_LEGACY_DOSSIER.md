# 6. The Long Biography

Saiffil Fariz’s story begins with curiosity.

As a child, he was given a Whiz Kid personal computer. To another child, it may have been an educational toy. To Saiffil, it became a doorway. It introduced the idea that a machine could respond, that instructions could create outcomes, and that there was a hidden logic behind what appeared on a screen.

That curiosity did not remain abstract.

From childhood, Saiffil became fascinated with how computers worked physically and logically. He dismantled and reassembled PCs. He installed operating systems and software through floppy disks, diskettes, CDs and DVDs. He learned by fixing problems, troubleshooting hardware, upgrading software, replacing components and experimenting alone until a system responded.

This period formed one of the deepest patterns in his life: do not wait passively for answers; study the system, test assumptions and find the solution.

By the mid-1990s, Saiffil was already building websites. The web became a natural continuation of his curiosity because it connected code, design, publishing, communication and opportunity. It was no longer only about making a computer work. It was about making things visible, useful and reachable by other people.

His formal technical path later took him to Computer Engineering at The University of Arizona. During that period, he developed a deep affinity for terminals, SSH and command-line environments. He learned C, C++ and Java, and later fell in love with PHP, HTML, CSS and JavaScript—the languages and tools that allowed him to rapidly turn ideas into usable web systems. The terminal taught him directness. Code taught him structure. The web taught him that technology becomes meaningful when people can use it.

In the early 2000s, Saiffil moved further into the internet economy. He explored affiliate models, studied the logic behind digital media and subscription businesses, traded through platforms such as eBay and Half.com, and founded masyuk.com in 2004. Through masyuk.com and related ventures, he entered global e-commerce, worked with payment gateways beginning with PayPal, and developed practical understanding of how digital products, payments, trust and distribution worked across borders.

At the same time, he experimented with servers and media distribution. He had his own dedicated server from around 2004 and also served music and media through FTP connections from a home PC. At one stage, users were downloading up to approximately 4 TB per month. The demand became so heavy that the machine overheated and damaged the processor. This was not a formal lesson in scalability. It was a lived lesson. He learned that popularity creates load, that systems must be built for demand, and that scale exposes limits that smaller experiments hide.

From there, Saiffil’s programming increasingly became tied to entrepreneurship. He did not write software only as an intellectual exercise. He built systems because his ventures needed them. He developed software for automation, delivery, feedback, payments, stock, finance, marketing, sales, accounts and operational workflows. This shaped his view that a business is also a system: it has inputs, outputs, bottlenecks, feedback loops, dependencies, risks and human behaviours.

This is one of the most important bridges in his life.

The programmer became the entrepreneur because the problems became larger.

Later, Saiffil became deeply associated with Maxxima and CellMaxx. In Maxxima, his work moved into organisational and commercial scale. The company and its network reportedly grew to hundreds of thousands of members and distributed large sums in bonuses and commissions. The exact figures and periods should be carefully verified before public use, but the larger meaning is clear: Saiffil experienced the realities of building systems that involved products, agents, incentives, partners, teams, branding, distribution and trust.

Through CellMaxx, the story expanded further into brand visibility and regional ambition. The CellMaxx brand became associated with bold campaigns, including branding on an AirAsia aircraft, presence within the aircraft environment and in-flight media, bus or transit branding, regional campaigns and sponsorships. The AirAsia aircraft story is especially significant because it represents Saiffil’s instinct for influence: not simply placing a logo somewhere, but designing repeated, large-scale visibility that people could not easily ignore.

For Saiffil, branding was not only artwork.

Branding was attention, memory, trust, movement and repeated encounter.

His wider journey also included media, affiliate models, Netflix-style thinking, YouTube, e-commerce, payment systems, marketplaces, regional campaigns, software platforms, training, community work and religious knowledge ecosystems. These were not disconnected interests. They formed a pattern: Saiffil kept studying how ideas travel, how people decide, how systems scale, how trust forms and how capability spreads.

Today, this entire history converges in AI.

Saiffil does not approach AI as a sudden new career. He sees it as the newest multiplier of capabilities that were already being formed across decades. AI connects with programming because it changes how systems are built. It connects with business because it changes how value is created. It connects with media because it changes how content is produced and distributed. It connects with learning because it changes how people explore knowledge. It connects with leadership because it changes decisions, risks and responsibilities. It connects with character because power without judgment can become dangerous.

His message is therefore not simply “use AI.”

Saiffil’s work now sits at the intersection of AI transformation, systems thinking, business building, digital capability, communication, learning and human development. He speaks, teaches, builds and advises from the perspective of someone who has lived through several waves of computing and internet evolution—not as a spectator, but as a participant.
